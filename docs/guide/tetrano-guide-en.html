Tetrano & Qubic 4×4×4: strong openings, stubborn defence

Which moves to play early in the game, and why. Everything here comes from a proven winning strategy, computed and checked against every possible defence.

The first player wins

If they never make a mistake, whatever the opponent does.

The second player can hold out

Good defence pushes back the moment the opponent's win becomes mechanical, while keeping your own chances.

The diagrams show the Tetrano board from above: 16 squares, 4 ring sizes per square. For Qubic 4×4×4, each ring size is one layer of the cube.

1. Strong places, weak places

There are 76 winning lines. A ring in a corner, small or large, belongs to 7 of them: here they are, in yellow.

Only 16 places lie on 7 lines: the small and large rings of the 4 corners, and the medium rings of the 4 central squares. All the others lie on only 4 lines.

The game has 192 symmetries: you can rotate and flip the board, reverse the order of the sizes, and even swap corners and centres without changing the game. As a result, up to symmetry there are only two kinds of places, strong and weak. From here on, your first ring is fixed in the bottom-left corner, the smallest one, and only one version of each position is shown.

2. When you move first

Move 1: a strong place

There are only two truly different first moves:

The proven strategy starts on a strong place: almost twice as many lines, hence more ways to make them cross later, which is the key to winning.

Move 3: a second corner, on a free line

The opponent has 12 truly different replies (all others are mirror images of these). Here is the strategy's move for each (label 3). It is always a corner, linked to your first ring by a line the opponent has not touched (in yellow).

“Positions of the strategy”: how many of the 2,519 positions where the strategy has to choose its move lie in this branch (one position can serve several branches). The replies in the centre take up the largest part of the strategy.

Why a corner? Two strong places joined by a free line: sooner or later the opponent must block it, often on a weak place. And two corners on the same side of the cube prepare the next move.

Move 5: the triangle

The third ring aims to be in line with both of the first two. Three corners on the same side of the cube are linked in pairs by three lines: the opponent can block only one per move.

In the strategy, the third ring is a corner in 78% of cases and a centre in 15%; it is in line with both of your first rings 8 times out of 10. If the opponent has already blocked the line between your two corners, take a corner in line with each of them along other lines:

Then: the threat sequence

A threat is three rings on a line whose fourth place is free: the opponent must block it. Chaining threats, you end up creating two at once: the fork, which cannot be stopped. As soon as you have enough crossing lines with two rings, the win becomes mechanical.

At every move, in this order: 1. win if you can; 2. otherwise block an opponent's threat; 3. otherwise look for a threat sequence ending in a fork; 4. only then, a building move.

3. When you move second

Against perfect play there is no defence. The best second-player strategy is therefore the one that loses as late as possible, giving the first player time to go wrong, and that, if they do, gives you real winning chances.

What we measure. Not the number of rings played: that would reward pointless threats played only to delay the end. We measure the moment the first player's win becomes mechanical (an unstoppable threat sequence), and how many moves they must find on their own before that. A threat of yours changes nothing on this count: their reply is forced.

Should you stay on strong places?

It is the natural intuition: keep your rings on 7-line places rather than settle inside the opponent's lines. It does not hold. A defence that only plays strong places lets the win become mechanical from move 9, whatever the reply at move 2 (second-to-last column of the table, and the annotated game in the previous chapter).

To see it more broadly, we went through the 358 positions of the strategy where the second player's choice matters. In each, they have about 57 possible moves on average, of which about 5 are “the best” (those that push back the mechanical win the most). We sorted all those moves into four kinds:

Kind of moveHow many per position (average)Share of these moves that are among the best

How to read it: a move that blocks a two-ring line is among the best one time in two, whether on a strong or a weak place. A strong place that blocks nothing is among the best only 7 times in 100. Strong places are not useless: when there is nothing to block, the best strong place is often as good as the best move (233 positions out of 358); but as soon as a two-ring line is open, it must be blocked. As for making a threat that leads nowhere, it rarely helps: only 13% of such moves are among the best.

The principles

  1. Take a centre at move 2.
  2. Block every two-ring line of the first player as soon as it appears. Don’t just sit on strong places.
  3. Pick the block that lines up with your own rings, ideally your centre: each move defends and prepares the counter-attack.
  4. Save your threats for a fork, or to block while threatening.
  5. When they force, keep building: each forced block can also extend your lines; if their threat sequence is not exact, your counter-attack is ready.

With the handicap, the first player wins 68% of the games and the second 1%; 30% end in a draw (full board). The network alone, without search, rated the position at 44% wins for the first player, 45% draws and 11% for the second.

Verdict: the handicap is not enough to balance the game. In theory the first player always wins (see the exact computation below). In practice the handicap changes a lot: without it, the first player wins 99% of the games, in 26 moves on average; with it, 68%, in 42 moves, and nearly one game in three goes on to a full board. But the second player almost never wins: their best prospect is the draw. Counting a win as 1 point and a draw as ½, the first player still scores 84% of the points. For a truly fair match, play games in pairs, swapping roles: each player starts once with the handicap, and the points scored decide.

The exact computation: the first player still wins

To go beyond statistics, the same computation as for the winning strategy without handicap was run on this position: an exact proof, examining every defence. After a first move on a weak place, the second player has 23 truly different replies (instead of 12 after a corner), shown here. All of them lose. The first player has a winning strategy despite the handicap: with perfect play on both sides, they win 100% of the games, as without the handicap.

This strategy was replayed on its own against every possible defence: 15,094,509 positions examined, games of at most 58 rings, no loss and no draw. It holds 30,376 positions where the right move must be known, against 2,519 without the handicap: the handicap makes the win about 12 times harder to memorise, and the games much longer, but not impossible.

It took 56 hours of computing. The last reply to fall was the corner next to the first ring: the search had dug into a single line over 40 moves deep; once corrected to try nearby alternatives first, it concluded in a minute.

The second player’s best reply

A deep search (20,000 simulations) ranks the replies by a score from −1 (the first player surely wins) to +1 (the second surely wins): the higher, the better for the second player.

As against a corner, the second player replies in the centre. Replies on weak places are clearly worse (score close to −1).

In the games played, the second player chose the best reply 392 times out of 400.

5. Summary

Moving first

  1. A strong place.
  2. Move 3: a corner linked to the first by a free line.
  3. Move 5: the triangle.
  4. Then: a threat sequence up to the fork.
  5. Always first: win if you can, otherwise block.

Moving second

  1. Move 2: a centre.
  2. Block every two-ring line as soon as it appears.
  3. Block while lining up with your own rings.
  4. No free threats.
  5. When they force, block while building.

To practise in the app, choose “Computer” as the one who begins: level 102 plays exactly this strategy (first ring on a strong place), level 103 the one proven after a first ring on a weak place (chapter 4). Neither ever loses or draws: try to hold out as long as you can.

Where this advice comes from

From Tetrano's forced-win graph (file tools/wingraph/graph/tetrano_win.bin in the repository): 2,519 positions with the move to play, checked by replaying the strategy against every defence. Everywhere else the strategy applies “win, block, threat sequence”.

For the second player, every position of this strategy was scored by how many moves the first player still has to find before their win becomes mechanical; the best defence maximises that number (on ties: a non-threatening move, then the app network's estimate). These measures hold against this winning strategy; a first player winning another way may call for other replies.
